## About

April Dunford is widely regarded as one of the leading authorities on product positioning for B2B technology companies.

After a 25-year career serving as VP of Marketing at multiple fast-growing technology firms, she transitioned into consulting and has worked with more than 300 technology companies, including Google, Epic Games, and Postman.

Her work focuses on helping companies make complex products easier to understand and easier to buy through stronger positioning, clearer differentiation, and better sales narratives.

She is the author of:

- Obviously Awesome

- Sales Pitch

Her thinking consistently emphasizes positioning as the foundation that drives messaging, sales, product marketing, and company strategy.

# Recent LinkedIn Post 1

## A market point of view matters more than ever in the AI era

April argues that companies need more than a product vision—they need a clear point of view about where their market is going.

Customers don't live inside technology the way founders and operators do. During periods of rapid technological change they actively look to vendors for guidance on what the future will look like.

She contrasts several examples:

### OpenAI

Sam Altman's view:

- AI will eventually be sold like electricity

- customers will consume intelligence on demand

- AI becomes utility infrastructure

This reflects a consumer-oriented view of the future.

### Anthropic

Dario Amodei's statement:

- AI will write 90% of code

This reflects a more B2B-oriented perspective centered around software development.

### Microsoft

Satya Nadella emphasizes:

- models become commodities

- business context becomes the differentiator

- enterprise data is the real intelligence layer

Each company reveals its positioning through its market point of view.

Key takeaway:

Companies should intentionally communicate their beliefs about where their market is heading rather than only describing their products.

# Recent LinkedIn Post 2

## Positioning in the Age of AI

April outlines several major positioning principles for AI-native and SaaS companies.

### 1. A point of view is now mandatory

Rapid market change creates uncertainty.

Customers increasingly expect vendors to explain:

- what is changing

- why it matters

- how they should prepare

Companies without a clear market perspective become harder to trust.

---

### 2. Position against today's competitors

Many founders become distracted by future competitive scenarios.

Product strategy can prepare for tomorrow's market.

Positioning should solve today's buying decision.

Positioning too far ahead of customer understanding creates confusion.

---

### 3. Connect today's reality with tomorrow's vision

Customers worry that:

- today's investments may become obsolete

- AI will rapidly change software categories

- current products may become legacy technology

Companies need both:

- an exciting future vision

- a believable migration path from today's reality

---

### 4. Customer understanding becomes even more important

During technology shifts it's easy for founders to become trapped inside industry hype.

April recommends systematic customer listening through:

- customer advisory boards

- executive sponsorship

- win/loss analysis

- ongoing customer research

Real customer thinking should continuously shape positioning decisions.

## Positioning is only the beginning

One of April's major themes is that companies often believe the positioning exercise is the difficult part.

In reality:

Positioning must be translated into:

- sales pitches

- messaging

- marketing assets

- customer conversations

before it creates business value.

---

## Positioning should be tested through sales conversations

April strongly argues against homepage A/B testing.

Homepage testing simultaneously measures:

- messaging

- copywriting

- design

- traffic quality

- CTA effectiveness

rather than positioning itself.

Instead she recommends:

- translate positioning into a sales pitch

- use top-performing sales reps

- pitch qualified prospects

- observe reactions live

Signals include:

- confusion

- excitement

- objections

- questions

- engagement

Customer conversations provide dramatically richer feedback than landing page experiments.

---

## Her sales pitch framework

The recommended structure:

1. Insight

Explain how the market works and provide context.

2. Alternatives

Discuss existing approaches and their tradeoffs.

3. Perfect world

Define what an ideal solution would look like.

4. Product introduction

Introduce the product and market category.

5. Differentiated value

Explain unique capabilities and why they matter.

6. Proof

Demonstrate credibility through customers or evidence.

7. Objection handling

Address likely concerns.

8. Ask

End with the desired next step.

## The messaging document philosophy

Rather than treating the homepage as the source of truth, April recommends maintaining a centralized messaging document.

It should include:

- official positioning

- differentiated value

- approved messaging

- company descriptions

- taglines

- feature explanations

- approved graphics

- customer quotes

- spokesperson bios

Every campaign should derive from this document to maintain consistency.

---

## Repetition builds positioning

One of her strongest recurring ideas:

Companies almost always abandon messaging too early.

Marketing teams become tired of hearing their own positioning long before customers have actually absorbed it.

Strong positioning requires repeating the same message consistently over long periods until it becomes associated with the brand.

As she says:

"It requires you to essentially say the same thing over and over and over again until you're really quite sick of it. Then at the point where you're quite sick of it is exactly where it's starting to land."

---

## Positioning should evolve only when reality changes

April recommends periodic positioning reviews every six months involving:

- CEO

- Head of Sales

- Head of Product

- Marketing

Review triggers include:

- major competitive changes

- product capability changes

- market shifts

- changing customer perception

Importantly:

Messaging should not change simply because the internal team is bored of it.

Only genuine positioning changes should drive messaging changes.
